Roman and I agreed to meet at the marina at 9:00 AM, with a rough plan to be out on the water for about three hours before lunch. As usual, plans changed.

## A Slow Start

When we got to the boat we ran into our neighbours on the motorboat next door. They were chatty as always, and Roman bore the brunt of the conversation. Distracted while preparing the jib, he forgot to thread the sheets through the blocks on the tracks — something we only discovered once the sail was already up. Meanwhile, the engine refused a cold start and needed a proper warm-up before it would run. Once warm it fired up without hesitation.

## Sorting Out the Jib

We motored out and raised the sails. A decent breeze was blowing. We started discussing where to go: I suggested Flakfortet, Roman said that was too far and proposed Lomma instead. I pointed out that even Lomma wasn't going to happen before lunch, and since we hadn't brought any food, we settled on stopping to eat there. A good goal — decided, done.

Then we tried to tension the jib and found something strange: the sheet kept jamming in the winch no matter how we tried to pull it in. I checked the wrap direction — clockwise, which is correct. Still jammed. Tried counter-clockwise on a hunch. Same thing. The angle the sheet was making toward the sail looked completely wrong, and I noticed the lead block on the rail was sitting unusually close to the cockpit. On closer inspection, the sheet wasn't running through that block at all.

We sorted it out: left the sail as it was, fed the sheet properly through the block on the other side, then tacked to fix the second side the same way. After that both sheets ran cleanly and the sails were trimmed correctly.

## Tacking North

With a northerly wind we had to work upwind to reach Lomma, which meant four or five tacks to get on a clean line toward the harbour. On the way we also tackled a small rigging job: the clew of the mainsail had been tied to the reefing line, which was never the right solution. We looked up how to tie a bowline — neither of us could remember it off the top of our heads — and Roman tied the clew off to the proper thick rope we'd been using for it last season. That fix paid off on the return leg when we needed to reef.

As we approached Lomma we noticed we weren't alone. Several other yachts were heading the same direction — we counted at least five. They were all on the same course, which gave it the feel of an impromptu regatta. Most of the others had better rigs and were faster, but we still managed to overtake a couple of them, which felt satisfying enough.

## Berthing in Lomma

We started dropping the sails about 300 metres out. Some boats behind us waited until they were right at the harbour entrance before doing the same, which led to a bit of chaos. The wind had strengthened by the time we arrived, which made things interesting.

We motored into the harbour and looked for a short berth that matched our mooring lines. We checked the right wing first — no luck. There were a few short spaces right at the entrance on the way in, but Roman ruled them out: poor visibility from the main channel made them risky. We turned around in the right wing, which was just about possible if you start from the far right and put the helm hard over. Then we checked the left wing — again, only long berths. Another tight turn.

In the end we chose a long berth and rigged the long mooring lines. The spot was perpendicular to the wind, so the boat was pushed sideways the moment we slowed down. It took three attempts — reversing, pushing off other boats, driving forward again — before Roman got us in. Once we were in the berth the mooring lines gave us more trouble: with just the two of us and the boat pulling against them, the standard method of dropping loops over cleats wasn't working. We switched to a cowboy approach, forming a lasso loop and throwing a self-tightening noose over each post. That worked.

While we were still getting settled, another yacht was coming in to the neighbouring berth. The skipper initially thought we were in his spot and waved us off, but then saw we were one berth over. He was single-handed, and the wind got the better of him on approach — his bow caught our pushpit rail stanchion. It left a small scratch and a slight dent. We checked the stanchion; it was still solid. Given that the excess damage wouldn't clear our insurance deductible anyway, we let it go. The skipper apologised at length and blamed the wind, which was fair enough. We asked him whether we needed to pay the harbour fee for a short daytime stop without staying overnight — he thought not, that the fee only applied to overnight stays.

## Ashore in Lomma

We locked up and went looking for somewhere to eat. We walked along the waterfront, took a detour to the beach and watched the kitesurfers for a while, then found a pleasant café right on the sand. Burgers, a proper rest, and then back to the boat.

## The Return

The engine started on the first crank. We cast off and headed out. Given that we'd be running more downwind than upwind on the way back, we took one reef in the mainsail and left the jib full, planning to use it more like a spinnaker in the following wind. I freed the sail ties from the cockpit and raised both sails without needing to go forward.

The northerly had built a proper chop along the strait, which made the autopilot struggle. We hand-steered the first half — Roman first, then Ivan — getting practice holding a steady course in the waves. After we crossed the shipping lane the conditions eased enough to hand over to the autopilot, which managed well enough, though it was working hard. The sailing itself was lively but comfortable: a reasonable heel, good speed, nothing alarming.

With the wind behind us we were making around 6 knots on average, and the run back took just over an hour.

To drop the sails we brought the boat head to wind. I made my way onto the bow to gather the jib — with the waves still running it was a bit like sitting on a mechanical bull, but nothing unsafe. I held on with one hand and pulled the sail down with the other. We dropped the mainsail after that and motored into the home harbour through the leftover chop.

The berthing at home — after the performance in Lomma — felt almost effortless.

## After Sailing

Neither of us was in a hurry, so we decided to make the most of it and rinse the boat down. We went over the whole hull with water and a brush. We also wanted to fill the fuel cans at the marina pump, but the payment terminal was out of order. We'll sort that another time. We shook hands and headed home.
